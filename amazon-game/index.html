The HQ2 Bid

Winning the Company—But Does the City Win?

An interactive case study inspired by Amazon’s search for a second headquarters.

A technology company is looking for a new headquarters. Its proposal projects 2,000 jobs within five years. Local businesses are already imagining new customers. Residents want to know who will get the jobs. Your finance director wants to know how much the city will have to give up to bring the company here.

You lead the city’s bid. The company can choose among several places, and other cities are preparing offers of their own. You can propose a subsidy, set conditions on payment, or walk away. The company decides where to build. You decide what you are willing to offer on the public’s behalf.

The city, offers, job targets, and outcomes in this simulation are fictional.

Round One · Make an Offer

The company’s representatives ask what your city can offer. Your city has a possible site, a workforce, and a transit network. These are reasons the company might choose your city even with a smaller subsidy. You have no way of knowing how the company ranks them against what other places can offer.

A local restaurant owner hopes the headquarters will bring customers throughout the week. A resident at the public meeting asks whether the advertised jobs will be open to people who already live here. Your finance director asks which other projects might have to wait if the city commits to a larger package.

You have to make an opening offer before you know what the other cities will bid.

At the next public meeting, one resident asks what you will do if the company chooses the rival. Another asks what happens if you win and the company creates only half the jobs it projected. Your next decision will affect both what the company can receive and what it has to deliver to receive it.

Who Gained What?

Your City Was Selected

The company chose your city in this simulation. It secured an offer worth up to $350 million, an increase of from your opening offer. The deal has no requirement tying payments to the 2,000-job projection.

The local government can announce the investment and take credit for securing the headquarters. It has also taken on a larger potential fiscal commitment. If hiring falls behind, the government has no hiring clause in this deal that automatically reduces payments in proportion to the shortfall.

Residents may benefit from new jobs or new customers for local businesses. To judge the deal, they would need to know how many jobs were actually added, what those jobs pay, how accessible they are to local workers, and how much public support the company ultimately receives. They would also need some basis for judging whether the company might have chosen the city with a smaller offer.

Five years later, the company has created 1,000 of the 2,000 jobs it projected. What evidence would you want before deciding whether the larger offer was worth it?

Your Offer Is Still on the Table

The company can accept a package worth up to . To receive the full amount, it would have to create 2,000 verified new jobs by the five-year mark. If it accepts and creates 1,000 qualifying jobs, it receives half the offer, or , under the simulation’s rules.

The local government has made the potential payout depend on something it can measure. It still has to define what counts as a job, check the company’s reporting, and decide how to handle positions that disappear soon after the deadline. The company can reject the terms and choose another place.

Residents can ask for a public record of the jobs that qualified and the amount paid. A job count answers one question. Pay, hours, job duration, and who actually gets hired answer others. Residents might also ask whether the payment rewards newly created jobs or positions the company moved from another office.

If a company meets its hiring target with mostly short-term positions, has it delivered what you wanted the subsidy to buy?

Your City Walked Away

The company cannot collect a subsidy from your city through this bid. It can still negotiate with the rival, choose another site, or reconsider the project.

The local government has preserved the resources it could have committed to the subsidy. It can now consider other uses, such as improving transport or supporting workforce training. It may also have to explain its decision to residents who wanted it to compete for the headquarters.

Residents can compare what the government does with those resources against the benefits the headquarters was expected to bring. That comparison comes with uncertainty. The city may never know whether it could have secured the investment at a lower price, and the projected jobs cannot tell residents who would actually have been hired.

What would make you more comfortable walking away: a credible plan for the money, stronger evidence about the company’s likely hiring, or an agreement with neighboring cities about how they compete?

The City Knows What It Has to Offer

The city now knows it has something the company values: a better location for its workforce. Its officials have more reason to hold their price and negotiate over hiring commitments. Your team can focus on getting the project underway.

The Rival Offer Gives You Room to Bargain

The city knows what the rival offered, but it still cannot tell how likely you are to go there. That uncertainty gives you room to ask for more. The city can raise its offer, insist on conditions, or walk away.

What Actually Happened?

In January 2018, Amazon said it had reviewed 238 proposals from across the United States, Canada, and Mexico and invited 20 metropolitan areas to the next stage of its headquarters search. In November, it announced two locations: Long Island City in Queens, New York, and Arlington, Virginia. Amazon projected more than 50,000 jobs across the two sites. At that point, the announcement told the public where Amazon planned to build and what it expected the projects to deliver. Amazon’s shortlist · Location announcement

Long Island City · Selected, Then Cancelled

New York’s memorandum of understanding outlined a headquarters project with 25,000 new jobs within ten years. Amazon’s announcement described a proposed package of up to $1.2 billion in refundable state tax credits and a $325 million state grant tied to building occupancy. The tax credits depended on payroll from qualifying new jobs. These figures describe potential incentives under the announced plan. New York memorandum · Location announcement

In February 2019, Amazon cancelled the planned Queens headquarters. The company cited opposition from some state and local politicians and said it needed working relationships with officials who would support the project over time. That statement gives Amazon’s account of the decision. The cancellation shows that securing an announcement did not settle whether the project could go ahead. It also makes the announced subsidy figures a poor measure of what the public actually paid for the planned headquarters. Amazon’s withdrawal statement

Arlington · Selected, With Payments Tied to Conditions

Arlington proceeded with HQ2. Its county grant tied payments to two conditions: Amazon had to meet office occupancy targets, and the county’s transient occupancy tax revenue, largely associated with hotel stays, had to exceed a specified baseline. Virginia’s state workforce grant was separate and used job creation as a basis for payment. The two levels of government used different measures of what the investment was expected to deliver. Location announcement · Arlington County statement

Arlington County reported that the first payment under its hotel tax grant came in fiscal year 2025. For fiscal year 2026, Amazon met its occupancy target, but hotel tax revenue fell below the baseline, so no payment was due under that grant for that period. A company can open its offices and fulfill one condition while a separate condition still determines whether public money changes hands. Arlington County statement

When you compare the locations, keep track of three events. Selection is where the company plans to locate. Commitment is what the public parties agree to make available under specified terms. Payment is what the company actually receives. A headline about who won tells you about the first event. To understand the costs and benefits, you need evidence about all three.

Who Had the Power to Set the Terms?

The Company Had Other Places to Go

The second round puts you in a position that Cailin Slattery studies in her model of subsidy competition. Governments offer public money to attract an investment, and the company can choose among locations. If several places are viable, the company can point to one offer while negotiating with another. Each government has to decide how far it will go with incomplete information about what the company would accept. In Slattery’s model, competition can shift the gains from the deal toward firms as governments compete for the same investment. Slattery

Think back to the $300 million rival offer. You saw a number before you knew how the company valued your workforce, transit, or site. If the company already preferred your city, raising the offer might mostly transfer more value to the company. If the rival was genuinely more attractive, raising it might change the decision. That is the question of additionality. What did the subsidy cause that would otherwise never have happened? The simulation gives you a way to face that uncertainty, while the historical records show which terms were publicly announced.

Officials Had Their Own Reasons to Bid

Nathan Jensen and his coauthors use a survey experiment to study how voters assign credit and blame to governors in a competition for investment. Their findings suggest that offering incentives can help an official take credit when an investment arrives and reduce the political fallout when it goes elsewhere. That creates a reason to bid even when the fiscal merits of a deal are uncertain. In the game, raising the offer gives the official a potential headline today. The residents who want to evaluate it may have to wait years for hiring figures and payment records. Jensen et al.

There are also economic reasons to compete. Michael Greenstone and his coauthors found that large manufacturing plants could raise productivity at existing plants nearby, especially where firms shared similar labor or technology. Their evidence concerns manufacturing, so an HQ2 analysis would have to look separately for benefits to surrounding businesses. It does help explain why an official might think about suppliers, workers, and other employers when deciding whether to bid. Slattery’s analysis then asks how much of any expected gain local governments can retain when they compete for it. Greenstone et al. · Slattery

Public Gains Have to Be Measured Over Time

Patrick Kline and Enrico Moretti’s study of the Tennessee Valley Authority offers a historical comparison. In that regional development program, manufacturing employment gains continued after federal transfers ended, while the initial gains in agricultural employment eventually reversed. A headquarters deal operates on a different scale, but the comparison gives us a way to evaluate its promises. If a city expects a subsidy to create lasting gains, it should identify what could persist when the payments end, such as stable employment or benefits to other local firms. Kline and Moretti

In 1943, Michał Kalecki argued that when employment depends heavily on private investment, business decisions can indirectly influence public policy. HQ2 puts that idea at the scale of a place competing for a headquarters. A firm controls where it invests. Public officials control what they offer and which terms they attach. Residents can ask officials to defend those terms and show what the company delivered. The agreement matters because it determines whose promises are measurable and who bears the cost if expectations prove wrong. Kalecki
